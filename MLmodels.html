1. Bootstrapping Principles (The Resampling Engine)

Figure 1: Non-Parametric Resampling with Replacement & OOB Partitioning Resampling Core
Figure 1: Generating $B$ replicated datasets of size $N$ with replacement from original empirical data.

Before exploring complex estimators, we examine the sampling engine that enables non-parametric variance reduction: Bootstrapping. Formally, given a training sample $\mathcal{D} = \{\mathbf{x}_1, \dots, \mathbf{x}_N\}$, bootstrapping constructs $B$ distinct empirical replicates $\mathcal{D}^*_1, \dots, \mathcal{D}^*_B$ by uniformly drawing $N$ observations with replacement.

Because selection occurs with replacement, any given observation has a fixed selection probability of $1/N$ on each draw. The probability that an arbitrary observation is never selected across $N$ draws converges asymptotically:

$$\lim_{N \to \infty} \left(1 - \frac{1}{N}\right)^N = \frac{1}{e} \approx 0.3679 \quad (36.8\%)$$

Consequently, approximately 63.2% of unique records appear in each training bag. The remaining 36.8% comprise the Out-Of-Bag (OOB) partition, providing a deterministic validation set without requiring cross-validation splits.

2. Linear & Logistic Regression (Parametric Foundations)

Figure 2: Continuous Hyperplane vs. Sigmoid Probability S-Curve Parametric Baseline
Figure 2: Comparing the continuous hyperplane of Linear Regression with the Sigmoidal $[0, 1]$ mapping of Logistic Regression.

Parametric methods establish foundational predictive benchmarks by assuming a defined mathematical form for the decision surface:

  • Linear Regression (Continuous Target): Models an unbounded real-valued target $y \in \mathbb{R}$ via Ordinary Least Squares (OLS):
    $$\mathcal{L}_{\text{OLS}}(\boldsymbol{\beta}) = \sum_{i=1}^n \left( y_i - \mathbf{x}_i^T \boldsymbol{\beta} \right)^2 = \|\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_2^2$$
    Minimizing this quadratic surface yields the closed-form normal equation: $\boldsymbol{\beta}^* = (\mathbf{X}^T \mathbf{X})^{-1} \mathbf{X}^T \mathbf{y}$.
  • Logistic Regression (Binary / Probability Target): Maps linear combinations to calibrated probabilities using the non-linear Sigmoid link function:
    $$P(y = 1 \mid \mathbf{x}) = \sigma(\mathbf{x}^T \boldsymbol{\beta}) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-\mathbf{x}^T \boldsymbol{\beta}}}$$
    Parameters are optimized via maximum likelihood, minimizing the convex Binary Cross-Entropy (Log-Loss):
    $$\mathcal{L}_{\text{BCE}}(\boldsymbol{\beta}) = -\frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n \left[ y_i \ln(\hat{p}_i) + (1 - y_i) \ln(1 - \hat{p}_i) \right]$$

3. Decision Trees (CART Algorithm)

Figure 3: Recursive Binary Partitioning Hierarchy CART
Figure 3: Hierarchical feature threshold splitting yielding orthogonal decision regions.

Moving beyond linear hyperplanes, Decision Trees (CART) construct non-parametric models by recursively dividing the feature space into axis-aligned rectangular blocks. Each split greedily evaluates every feature $j$ and boundary $s$ to maximize purity gain:

  • Classification (Gini Impurity): Measures the likelihood that a random sample is mislabeled:
    $$I_G(p) = 1 - \sum_{k=1}^K p_k^2$$
  • Regression (Variance Reduction): Minimizes total squared residual error across child nodes:
    $$\mathcal{L}_{\text{split}} = \sum_{i \in R_L} (y_i - \bar{y}_{R_L})^2 + \sum_{i \in R_R} (y_i - \bar{y}_{R_R})^2$$

The Variance Bottleneck: Individual decision trees have low bias but high variance; minor data shifts can trigger structural alterations throughout the hierarchy, leading to overfitting.

4. Bagging & Random Forests (Variance Reduction)

Figure 4: Parallel Tree Aggregation & Subspace De-correlation Ensemble
Figure 4: Training $B$ de-correlated trees in parallel over random row and feature subsets to reduce variance.

By combining Bootstrapping (Section 1) with Decision Trees (Section 3), Bagging trains $B$ unpruned trees across bootstrap replicates and averages their predictions:

$$\hat{f}_{\text{bag}}(\mathbf{x}) = \frac{1}{B} \sum_{b=1}^B \hat{f}_b(\mathbf{x})$$

The Random Forest Breakthrough: In standard bagging, dominant predictors appear at the root of every tree, leaving the ensemble members correlated. Leo Breiman resolved this through Feature Subspace Sampling: evaluating only a random subset of $m$ features ($m \approx \sqrt{p}$) at every split.

$$\text{Var}\left(\frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^B X_b\right) = \rho \sigma^2 + \frac{1 - \rho}{B} \sigma^2$$

Reducing the inter-tree correlation coefficient $\rho$ lowers overall ensemble variance toward $\rho \sigma^2$ without inflating model bias.

5. The Boosting Paradigm: Sequential Learning

While Bagging fits independent models in parallel to reduce variance, Boosting constructs an additive sequence of weak models to systematically minimize bias:

$$F_M(\mathbf{x}) = \sum_{m=1}^M \gamma_m h_m(\mathbf{x})$$

Instead of voting independently, each successive base estimator $h_m(\mathbf{x})$ (typically a shallow decision stump) is trained to fit the residual errors of the existing ensemble $F_{m-1}(\mathbf{x})$.

6. Adaptive Boosting (AdaBoost)

Figure 5: AdaBoost Sequential Error Reweighting Sequential
Figure 5: Misclassified instances receive higher weights at each stage, guiding later learners toward difficult cases.

AdaBoost updates sample weights dynamically across training rounds:

  1. Calculate the weighted error rate for weak learner $h_m(\mathbf{x})$:
    $$\epsilon_m = \frac{\sum_{i: y_i \ne h_m(\mathbf{x}_i)} w_i^{(m)}}{\sum_{i=1}^n w_i^{(m)}}$$
  2. Derive the model authority weight $\alpha_m$:
    $$\alpha_m = \frac{1}{2} \ln \left( \frac{1 - \epsilon_m}{\epsilon_m} \right)$$
  3. Update sample weights:
    $$w_i^{(m+1)} = w_i^{(m)} \exp\left( -\alpha_m y_i h_m(\mathbf{x}_i) \right)$$

Final predictions aggregate all weighted weak learners: $H(\mathbf{x}) = \text{sign}\left(\sum_{m=1}^M \alpha_m h_m(\mathbf{x})\right)$.

7. Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost)

Unlike AdaBoost's sample reweighting, Gradient Boosting trains trees directly on negative loss gradients (pseudo-residuals):

$$r_{im} = -\left[ \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}(y_i, F(\mathbf{x}_i))}{\partial F(\mathbf{x}_i)} \right]_{F=F_{m-1}}$$

XGBoost builds on this framework through second-order optimization and structural regularization:

  • Second-Order Taylor Approximation: Leverages first-order gradients $g_i$ and second-order Hessians $h_i$:
    $$\tilde{\mathcal{L}}^{(t)} \approx \sum_{i=1}^n \left[ g_i f_t(\mathbf{x}_i) + \frac{1}{2} h_i f_t^2(\mathbf{x}_i) \right] + \Omega(f_t)$$
  • Explicit Regularization ($\Omega$): Directly limits tree complexity to prevent overfitting:
    $$\Omega(f_t) = \gamma T + \frac{1}{2} \lambda \sum_{j=1}^T w_j^2$$
  • Sparsity-Aware Routing: Learns default branch directions for missing data during split finding.
  • Hardware Acceleration: Uses cache-aware block memory structures and compressed column formats for parallel execution.

8. Meta-Ensemble Voting & Stacking

Meta-ensembles combine diverse algorithm families (linear, tree-based, and boosting models) to balance their individual inductive biases:

  • Hard Voting: Takes the majority class prediction across base estimators.
  • Soft Voting: Averages class probability outputs across well-calibrated classifiers:
    $$\hat{y} = \arg\max_k \sum_{m=1}^M w_m P_m(y=k \mid \mathbf{x})$$
  • Stacked Generalization (Stacking): Generates out-of-fold predictions to create a second-level feature matrix $\mathbf{Z} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times M}$, which a meta-estimator (e.g., ElasticNet or Logistic Regression) uses to learn optimal blending weights.

9. Convolutional Neural Networks (CNN)

Figure 6: Convolution, Pooling, and Dense Classification Stages Deep Vision
Figure 6: Feature extraction through convolution and pooling layers, followed by dense layers for class assignment.

Unlike tabular models that treat inputs as flat vectors, CNNs preserve spatial relationships in multi-dimensional grid data (such as image tensors) through localized operations:

1. Discrete 2D Convolution

Slides learnable filter kernels across spatial dimensions to detect shift-invariant patterns:

$$(I * K)(i, j) = \sum_{m} \sum_{n} I(i - m, j - n) K(m, n)$$

2. Spatial Pooling

Reduces spatial resolution while preserving dominant activations:

$$P(i, j) = \max_{m, n} A(i \cdot s + m, j \cdot s + n)$$

3. Dense Classification

Normalizes outputs into class probabilities via Softmax:

$$P(y = c \mid \mathbf{x}) = \frac{e^{z_c}}{\sum_{k} e^{z_k}}$$

10. Unsupervised Clustering: $k$-Means & Centroid Partitioning

Figure 7: k-Means Centroid Convergence & Voronoi Cell Partitions Unsupervised Geometry
Figure 7: Partitioning $N$ unlabeled instances into $k=3$ clusters by minimizing distances to cluster centroids $\boldsymbol{\mu}_k$.

Unlike the supervised models covered previously, $k$-Means Clustering operates on unlabeled data $\{\mathbf{x}_i\}_{i=1}^n$, discovering natural clusters by partitioning points into $k$ distinct Voronoi cells.

The Mathematical Objective: Inertia Minimization

The algorithm finds centroids $\{\boldsymbol{\mu}_1, \dots, \boldsymbol{\mu}_k\}$ that minimize the Within-Cluster Sum of Squares (WCSS):

$$\mathcal{J}(C, \boldsymbol{\mu}) = \sum_{j=1}^k \sum_{\mathbf{x}_i \in S_j} \|\mathbf{x}_i - \boldsymbol{\mu}_j\|_2^2$$

Lloyd's algorithm alternates between two steps until centroid positions stabilize:

  1. Assignment Step: Assign each point $\mathbf{x}_i$ to its nearest Euclidean centroid:
    $$S_j^{(t)} = \left\{ \mathbf{x}_i : \|\mathbf{x}_i - \boldsymbol{\mu}_j^{(t)}\|_2^2 \le \|\mathbf{x}_i - \boldsymbol{\mu}_l^{(t)}\|_2^2 \quad \forall l \right\}$$
  2. Update Step: Recalculate centroids as the mean of their assigned points:
    $$\boldsymbol{\mu}_j^{(t+1)} = \frac{1}{|S_j^{(t)}|} \sum_{\mathbf{x}_i \in S_j^{(t)}} \mathbf{x}_i$$

Selecting Optimal $k$ (Elbow vs. Silhouette)

1. The Elbow Method

Identifies the inflection point where additional clusters yield diminishing returns in explained variance (WCSS).

2. Silhouette Analysis

Measures cohesion against nearest neighbor separation for each point:

$$s(i) = \frac{b(i) - a(i)}{\max(a(i), b(i))}, \quad s(i) \in [-1, 1]$$
Scaling Requirement: Because $k$-Means computes isotropic Euclidean distances, features with large scales dominate smaller-scale dimensions. Features must be standardized (zero mean, unit variance) before computing distances.
